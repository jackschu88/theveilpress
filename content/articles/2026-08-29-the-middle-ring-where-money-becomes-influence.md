## Where money becomes influence

For years, Americans have been encouraged to imagine political power as a contest between two obvious forces: government on one side and voters on the other. But modern power rarely moves that directly.

Between the person who provides the money and the person who ultimately encounters the idea sits an enormous institutional layer — foundations, nonprofits, advocacy organizations, think tanks, universities, media organizations, financial institutions, and other intermediaries. Call it the middle ring. It is neither government nor the individual citizen. And increasingly, it is where enormous amounts of money and influence travel.

That makes the Treasury Department's current scrutiny of tax-exempt organizations worth watching — not simply because the administration is targeting organizations associated with the political left, but because it raises a much larger question:

**How transparent should the institutions between money and political influence be?**

## Follow the money — but don't stop there

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said in July that Treasury was expanding efforts to identify organizations that abuse charitable structures for illicit finance, foreign influence, or activity connected to violence.

His formulation is important. He did not say every nonprofit is corrupt. In fact, he explicitly acknowledged that charities and nonprofits represent an important part of civil society. His argument was that the very trust placed in those institutions can make them attractive vehicles for abuse.

That distinction matters. Because:

- A nonprofit receiving money from a wealthy donor is not evidence of wrongdoing.
- A foundation funding advocacy is not automatically evidence of a conspiracy.
- Foreign money entering an American nonprofit is not automatically illegal.

The question is what happens after the money enters the institution.

> Who receives it? Who controls it? Where does it go next? What activities does it support? What relationships exist between organizations? And what, if anything, is being hidden from the public?

Those are answerable questions. And they are far more interesting than simply asking whether George Soros is involved.

## The middle ring is not inherently sinister

This is where the discussion often goes wrong. The middle ring can perform an incredibly valuable function.

A foundation can fund cancer research. A nonprofit can provide disaster relief. A think tank can conduct legitimate research. A university can educate. An advocacy organization can represent citizens who otherwise wouldn't have political influence. A fiscal sponsor can allow a new organization to operate before it establishes its own legal infrastructure. These institutions are part of a healthy civil society.

But the same infrastructure that makes civil society powerful also makes it capable of concentrating influence. That's the part worth studying.

Treasury's April transparency initiative specifically focused on government grants, government contracts, and fiscal-sponsorship arrangements in Form 990 reporting, arguing that clearer information would help detect misconduct and misuse of taxpayer resources. That is not a conspiracy theory. That's an accounting question.

## The network matters more than the name

This is perhaps the most important lesson. Consider the chain:

**Donor → Foundation → Nonprofit → Fiscal Sponsor → Advocacy Organization → Media → Public**

No individual organization necessarily has to control the entire process. The influence can emerge from the network itself. Money moves. Personnel move. Research moves. Ideas move. Organizations fund other organizations. One institution provides legitimacy to another. Another provides the platform. Another provides the policy research. Another communicates the conclusion to millions of people.

And eventually the original source of the money can become almost invisible to the person receiving the final message. That doesn't prove wrongdoing. But it does demonstrate why institutional mapping matters.

## This is where the story becomes bigger than Trump

The temptation is to make this partisan — "Trump is finally going after the left," or "Trump is weaponizing the government against his political enemies." Both narratives are politically convenient. Neither is sufficient.

The deeper question is: what happens when the state begins examining the middle ring itself? Because once government begins looking seriously at nonprofit networks, the precedent doesn't belong exclusively to one political party. A Republican administration can investigate organizations associated with Democrats. A Democratic administration could someday investigate organizations associated with Republicans.

The machinery doesn't care who occupies the White House. Institutions persist longer than administrations. That is why the architecture matters.

## The precedent cuts both ways

There is another reason to pay attention. Federal tax law already places significant restrictions on 501(c)(3) organizations. Charities are prohibited from participating in political campaigns on behalf of or against candidates, and lobbying cannot constitute more than an insubstantial part of their activities.

So the legitimate question isn't: "Should nonprofits be allowed to have political opinions?" Of course they should. The question is: "At what point does an organization receiving a significant tax preference cease operating within the boundaries that justify that preference?"

That's a legal and institutional question. And it should be answered with evidence — not political loyalty.

## The real lesson

This is precisely why the study of institutions matters. Power rarely announces itself as power. It often appears as a grant. A tax exemption. A foundation. A research fellowship. A consulting contract. A fiscal sponsorship. A regulatory exemption. A charitable deduction. A board appointment. A relationship between two organizations that appear unrelated until someone bothers to map them.

None of these things is inherently corrupt. But each is a connection. And connections are how institutional power becomes visible. That is the lesson of the middle ring.

## The Archivist's question

The Archivist doesn't begin by saying: "These people are criminals." He begins somewhere much more dangerous:

> "Show me the records."

Show me the Form 990. Show me the donor. Show me the grant. Show me the intermediary. Show me the board. Show me the recipient. Show me the money. Show me the relationship. And then let the evidence determine what the relationship means.

Because if wrongdoing exists, the records should expose it. And if wrongdoing doesn't exist, the records should expose that too. That is the difference between investigation and conspiracy.

## The connection to The Veil of the Square Mile

The book's broader argument isn't that every institution hiding behind layers of law and finance is secretly corrupt. It's that you cannot understand power by looking only at its most visible face. You have to map the institutions surrounding it.

The City of London is interesting for precisely this reason: its historical significance isn't dependent on an imaginary secret council. Its actual institutional structure, legal privileges, financial relationships, and continuity are interesting enough to investigate on their own. The same principle applies here.

Don't invent the network. Map the network. And when the map reveals something uncomfortable, follow it.

That is the middle ring. And perhaps the most important question isn't whether the Trump administration is right about every organization it is investigating. It's whether America is finally beginning to look seriously at the institutions between money and power — and whether we're prepared to apply the same scrutiny regardless of which political tribe benefits from the answer.

Because the middle ring doesn't belong to the left or the right. It belongs to whoever learns how to use it.
