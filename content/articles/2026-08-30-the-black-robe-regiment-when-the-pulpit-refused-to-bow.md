Revolutions do not begin when the first shot is fired. They begin when ordinary people decide that the world around them is no longer morally acceptable — and before a people can resist power, they have to learn to see it.

That is where the pastors came in. Long before independence was declared, ministers throughout the American colonies were asking questions that were not supposed to be asked: What is the proper limit of government? When does obedience become submission to injustice? Does God require a Christian to obey every authority, no matter what it does? What happens when the ruler himself becomes the violator of the law?

These were not abstract questions. They were the questions of an empire. And from pulpits across the colonies, ministers began giving answers.

The British called them troublemakers. History remembers something like a regiment.

## The legend is not the point. The record is.

The phrase "Black Robe Regiment" has become part of American political mythology. It conjures an image of ministers in black clerical robes preaching liberty before marching their congregations into revolution. The reality is more complicated.

There was no single military regiment of pastors, no unified organization with a headquarters and a list of members. The clergy themselves were divided: some supported independence, some remained loyal to the Crown, some tried to avoid the conflict entirely. Even the famous story of Peter Muhlenberg finishing a sermon, throwing off his clerical robe, and revealing a military uniform beneath it has been treated by historians with caution.

But a simplified legend does not make the underlying story less important — it makes the real story more important. Religion played a substantial role in the American Revolution. Ministers preached, wrote, organized, served as chaplains, participated in legislatures and conventions, and helped give ordinary colonists a moral vocabulary through which to understand resistance to British rule. The Library of Congress describes religion as providing moral sanction for opposition to Britain and notes that ministers served the revolutionary cause in numerous capacities, including military service.

So the important question is not whether there were exactly three hundred ministers in some mythical regiment. The important question is why Christian leaders were willing to treat political power as a **moral question**. A significant number refused to accept the idea that God cared about private morality but had nothing to say about public power. The distinction matters. It may matter now more than ever.

## Before the revolution came the sermon

For generations, English and colonial Christians wrestled with a question that would eventually become explosive: **When is resistance to authority justified?**

The Bible commands Christians to respect governing authorities. Romans 13 has been quoted for centuries by kings, emperors, and ordinary governments alike. But the Bible also contains prophets confronting kings — Nathan confronting David, Elijah confronting Ahab, John the Baptist confronting Herod — and the apostles declared there were circumstances in which obedience to God took precedence over obedience to men.

So the question was never simply whether Christians should obey authority. It was: **What happens when authority itself becomes lawless?**

One influential voice was the Boston minister Jonathan Mayhew. In a sermon delivered on the anniversary of the execution of Charles I, Mayhew rejected the idea that Christians were morally required to submit without resistance to tyranny, arguing that resistance to an oppressive ruler could itself be a Christian duty.

This was dangerous thinking — not because it encouraged chaos, but because it forced people to distinguish between **authority** and **the abuse of authority**. A king could be lawful. A government could be legitimate. But neither title was a blank check. Power could become corrupt. Power could violate the very law it claimed to enforce. And when ordinary Christians could recognize that fact, the government was no longer God, the king was no longer beyond judgment, and the empire was no longer permanent.

## The pulpit was a communications network

The eighteenth century had no internet. But it had networks — and one of the most important in colonial America was the church.

The minister stood before a community every week. He interpreted the Scriptures, but he also interpreted the world: suffering, war, justice, and what events beyond the village meant to the people in the pews. The pulpit was not merely a place where people escaped reality for an hour; it was one of the places where reality was given meaning. The British understood this. So did the revolutionaries.

The Library of Congress describes ministers as helping transform colonial resistance into a morally justified cause, while clergy participated in the movement as chaplains, writers, legislators, and — in some cases — soldiers. That did not mean every sermon was political. It meant something more important: when political power became a moral question, **the church did not pretend that morality ended at the church door.**

## They did not begin by worshipping America

This is where the story is often corrupted. The lesson of the revolutionary clergy is not that pastors should turn their churches into campaign offices. The church is not an extension of a political party, and the pulpit is not a branch office for any faction. Christ did not come to build a voting bloc.

The point is deeper. The revolutionary pastors understood something many modern pastors seem to have forgotten: **Every government makes moral claims.** Every political system has an idea of what man is. Every law assumes something about justice. Every institution distributes power. Every empire eventually develops a story about why its power is necessary.

Whenever Christians are told they must not concern themselves with these things, they are not being asked to remain neutral. They are being asked to let someone else do the interpreting. That is not neutrality. That is surrender.

## The old empire did not disappear

The American Revolution broke political ties with Britain. But it did not erase the institutions that had helped build the British world — and that is one of the questions at the center of *The Veil of the Square Mile*.

The City of London is not simply another name for London. It is not merely a metaphor for bankers. It is a distinct municipal corporation with its own long institutional history, ancient rights and privileges, courts and governing structures, and a political identity that developed long before the modern British state existed in its current form. The City of London Corporation traces elements of its constitutional history through charters and liberties reaching back centuries, preserving documents that reach to the eleventh century.

That does not mean the City quietly controls the world. That is not the argument. The truth is more interesting than a cartoon.

**Institutions survive.** They adapt. They change their language, their personnel, their public image. But structures can outlive the generations that created them. A charter written for a medieval world can still matter centuries later. A corporation can survive kings. A legal privilege can outlast an empire. A financial network can change flags without disappearing. And eventually people stop seeing the structure — it becomes part of the scenery.

That is what *The Veil* is about: not secret meetings or imaginary cabals, but the record — the charters, the corporations, the laws, the institutions, the mechanisms through which power survives long enough to become invisible. The documents are not necessarily hidden. The problem is that almost nobody reads them.

## This is where the pastors come in

There is a strange modern doctrine quietly taking hold in many churches, usually expressed as: **"The church should stay out of politics."**

At first, that sounds wise. Sometimes it is. The church should not become a tribal headquarters, and pastors should not reduce the Gospel to a campaign slogan. But the phrase becomes dangerous when "politics" expands to mean everything involving public morality — justice, law, war, education, money, family, power, the dignity of human beings, the limits of the state, the corruption of institutions, the rights of parents, the moral consequences of economic systems, the question of who is allowed to rule and by what authority.

If all of that is declared "political," and the pastor is forbidden from discussing any of it, what exactly is left? A Christianity with no opinion about power. A Christianity that can speak about heaven but never about the institutions shaping life on earth. A Christianity that condemns personal sin while refusing to ask whether powerful institutions have become engines of corruption — telling the congregation "you are responsible for your private morality" while telling institutions above them "we have no comment."

That is not the historic posture of the prophets. It was not the posture of John the Baptist. And it was not the posture of the ministers who helped Americans think through the moral crisis of the eighteenth century.

## The problem with silence

Tyranny does not always arrive wearing a crown. Sometimes it arrives as a regulation, a financial mechanism, a court decision, an emergency, a new definition of what people are allowed to believe. Sometimes it comes with a smile and a slogan about safety. Sometimes it comes through an institution so old that nobody remembers who gave it its power.

And sometimes tyranny does not need the church to support it — it only needs the church to remain silent.

This is why the Black Robe Regiment matters. Not because we need pastors putting on costumes, and not because every pastor should run for office. The lesson is that there was a time when Christian leaders understood that **silence about power is itself a decision about power.** When the state crossed moral boundaries, some pastors did not say "that is political; we only discuss spiritual matters here." They understood that justice was spiritual, liberty was spiritual, tyranny was spiritual, truth was spiritual. There was no clean line where the responsibility of the Christian ended and the machinery of the world began.

## The descendants of the revolution are being told not to ask questions

There is an irony here. Americans celebrate a revolution against an empire while increasingly being taught not to question the systems that govern them — to learn history but not investigate institutions, to celebrate liberty but not ask who exercises power, to honor the Founders but not examine the world from which the Founding emerged, to quote the Declaration but not study the imperial and financial structures that shaped the Atlantic world around it.

And pastors are often encouraged to reinforce this silence. Stay out of it. Do not upset anyone. Do not talk about government, or power, or institutions. Preach the Gospel — as though the Gospel has nothing to say about truth, as though Christ's command to love one's neighbor has nothing to say about systems capable of harming millions, as though the prophets never addressed kings, as though John the Baptist never named Herod, as though the apostles never had to decide whether Caesar's authority had limits.

The demand for silence is often presented as humility. Sometimes it is simply fear — fear of losing members, offending donors, being called political, being misunderstood, discovering that people in the pews disagree. But leadership has never been the art of avoiding discomfort. The pastors of the revolutionary era were not remembered because they were universally popular. They were remembered because some of them understood the moment they were living in.

## The veil must be lifted again

The task before Christians is not to recreate 1776. History does not work that way. The task is not to manufacture a new "Black Robe Regiment." We do not need a costume — we need the courage that existed beneath it.

The courage to investigate. The courage to speak carefully. The courage to admit when the evidence is insufficient. The courage to say, **"I was wrong."** The courage to resist propaganda from our enemies — and, much harder, from our allies. The courage to follow the record wherever it leads.

Tyranny depends on force, money, and people willing to enforce its rules. But it also depends on something quieter: people not looking too closely, citizens accepting the story they are given, pastors believing that power is not their concern, Christians separating their moral convictions from the world in which those convictions must actually be lived.

The revolutionaries did not defeat the British Empire because every colonist was a soldier. Most were not. But ideas moved through communities. Arguments became convictions. Convictions became action. And ministers — along with writers, merchants, farmers, soldiers, legislators, and ordinary citizens — helped shape the moral world in which resistance became imaginable.

Before people can act, they have to understand what they are looking at. Before they can resist tyranny, they have to recognize it. Before they can recognize it, someone has to be willing to pull back the veil.

## Pastors: this is your moment

Not to become politicians. To become shepherds again.

A shepherd does not merely tell the sheep that wolves exist. He watches the field. He learns the terrain. He knows where danger comes from. And when something enters the field, he does not dismiss it because discussing wolves might be controversial — he tells the truth.

Pastors, your people are living inside systems they do not understand. Their children are being formed by institutions they cannot name. Their labor moves through financial structures they have never been taught to examine. Their beliefs are shaped by media organizations, governments, corporations, universities, algorithms, and bureaucracies that often understand the mechanics of influence far better than the average Christian understands the institutions surrounding his own life.

Too often, when Christians ask "What is happening?", the church answers "That is politics." No. Sometimes it is politics — and politics is simply one of the places where human beings exercise power over one another. Power is a moral subject. It always has been.

The question is not whether pastors should become politicians. The question is whether pastors should continue surrendering the moral interpretation of public life to people who do not share their responsibility to truth.

The men sometimes remembered as the Black Robe Regiment did not all agree. They were not a secret order. They were not a single army. They were flawed men living in a complicated moment. But many understood something we cannot afford to forget:

> **A free people must be capable of seeing the powers that govern them.**

And a church that refuses to help people see eventually leaves them to be guided by whoever is willing to tell them where to look.

That is why history matters. That is why institutions matter. That is why the old charters matter. That is why the City of London matters. That is why *The Veil of the Square Mile* begins with a place most people have heard of but almost nobody has been taught to understand.

## How does power survive?

The Square Mile is not the whole story. It is a doorway into a larger question: How does power survive? How does it move? How does it adapt? How does it become normal? And at what point do ordinary people stop seeing it?

Those are not conspiracy questions. They are historical questions. And history is full of warnings for people willing to read it.

The pastors who helped shape the moral world of the American Revolution did not possess some magical ability Christians have lost. They read. They argued. They studied. They preached. They looked at the powers of their day and refused to believe those powers were beyond moral judgment.

That is what must return. Not the costumes. Not the mythology. Not the partisan theater. The courage. The discipline. The willingness to stand before a congregation and say: **Look.** Look at the record. Look at the institution. Look at the law. Look at the history. Look at who benefits. Look at what has survived. Look at what has changed — and what has not. And then decide what truth requires of you.

That is how the veil begins to lift. And perhaps that is where the next movement begins too.
