## Two different places

Most people use "the City" and "London" as if they mean the same thing. They don't.

Greater London is a region of nearly nine million people, run by a mayor and an assembly under the laws of the United Kingdom. The City of London — the Square Mile — is something else: a roughly one-square-mile jurisdiction in the heart of the capital with its own corporation, its own electoral system, its own police force, and institutional continuity that predates the modern British state.

That is not a conspiracy theory. It is municipal fact.

## A governing body, not a nickname

The City of London Corporation is not a nickname for bankers. It is a governing body. It elects a Lord Mayor. It maintains the Court of Common Council and the Court of Aldermen. It operates the City of London Police — a force distinct from the Metropolitan Police that covers the rest of the capital. Its franchise has long included a business vote alongside residential suffrage, a structure almost no other democratic locality still defends in the same way.

None of this is hidden in the sense of a locked vault. It is written into charters, statutes, standing orders, and the Corporation's own published materials. What is rare is sustained public attention. The Square Mile is treated as scenery — a skyline, a synonym for "finance" — rather than as a political unit with its own rules of membership and authority.

## Why the gap matters

When a jurisdiction sits at the center of global capital flows and retains medieval-to-early-modern corporate forms inside a modern nation-state, the interesting question is not whether something "secret" is going on. The interesting question is how parallel authority was preserved, adapted, and normalized until most citizens stopped seeing it as authority at all.

Empire, banking, insurance, commercial law, and the geography of power in the North Atlantic did not float free of institutions. They ran through them. The Square Mile was one of those institutions — small on a map, large in the architecture of money and rule.

If you only ever read "the City" as a metaphor, you will miss the corporation. If you only ever read the corporation as a cartoon of villains, you will miss the record. The record is where the story actually is.
